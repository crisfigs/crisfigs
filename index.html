About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at CREED, University of Amsterdam, and the Tinbergen Institute, advised by Joël van der Weele and Jeroen van de Ven.

My research is in behavioral and experimental economics. I use online and lab-in-the-field experiments, together with microeconomic theory, to study how narratives, emotions, and memory shape beliefs, identity, and economic and political behavior.

Publications

Inconvenient Truths: A Note on Information Avoidance and the Price of Fairness

With Joël van der Weele

European Economic Review, 2026, 181: 105188

Abstract
Previous literature has shown that people are often reluctant to learn whether individually profitable actions have negative consequences for others. In an experimental allocation decision, we vary the ‘inconvenience’ of becoming informed about the payoffs of another player by changing the costs and benefits of choosing the fair outcome. Lowering the cost of fairness turns out to have a multiplier effect, raising both altruistic choices of informed subjects and the fraction of subjects that chooses to become informed. Thus, in situations of uncertainty, subsidizing altruistic choices to decision makers is an effective tool for raising social welfare. By contrast, variations in the size of recipients’ potential benefit have a smaller and insignificant effect on ignorance and fair choices.

Altruism as a Self-Control Problem

With Jantsje Mol, Ivan Soraperra, and Joël van der Weele

R&R, Journal of the European Economic Association

Abstract
Social preferences depend on emotional states like compassion and anger. Since emotions are fleeting and subject to manipulation, they may generate demand for commitment. We investigate the use of commitment strategies in an online experiment (n = 1,400), where subjects decide to watch or avoid videos before engaging in a charitable giving task. We find that a video with emotional content increases giving, but is also avoided more than non-emotional videos. We estimate a structural model of state-dependent social preferences, and show evidence for sophisticated commitment to selfishness and altruism. We argue that giving can be fruitfully analyzed as a self-control problem.

Which Motive Gets Crowded Out? Social Preferences and Conservation Incentives

With David Echeverry and Sandra Polanía Reyes

R&R, Ecological Economics

Abstract
A common pool resource game played by 705 villagers, whose livelihood depends on an actual resource across eight sites in Colombia, lets us ask which social motive a conservation incentive displaces. We estimate a latent class model on ten rounds played before any policy is announced, sorting participants into four posited preference types. Preferences for equitable outcomes dominate the population, but the type that restrains itself most is the altruist. We then interact each participant’s pre-treatment type probabilities with the taxes and subsidies randomly assigned to groups in ten rounds that follow. The tax reduces extraction by 19.0 percentage points of the maximum and displaces no motive we can detect. The subsidy reduces it by 20.8 points and displaces all three other-regarding motives: every ten points of probability of holding one of them offsets 7 to 12 points of its effect.
How Conflict Narratives and Intergenerational Memory Shape Political Behavior

With Egor Bronnikov

Abstract
We examine how narratives about a past conflict influence present-day political preferences and how they interact with intergenerationally transmitted family memories. In an online experiment in Spain (n = 2,593), participants read a far-right (Nationalist) or a centrist (Balanced) narrative about the Spanish Civil War, or an apolitical control text, with or without prior activation of family memories. They then allocate a real-stakes donation between a left- and a right-wing organization. Most participants (74%) report family-transmitted memories of the conflict. Among them, the Nationalist narrative increases donations to the right by 18% relative to the Balanced narrative. The effect is concentrated among right-leaning participants and is not amplified (if anything, it is weakly attenuated) when their memories are activated. Among left-leaning participants, activating memories halves donations to the right in the control condition, but not under either conflict narrative. Overall, narratives and memories shift behavior toward one's political ingroup when congruent with it, but offset each other's effects when they conflict. Mechanism analyses suggest that these effects operate through group identity: affect toward historical groups appears to transfer to their present-day counterparts, increasing affective polarization toward contemporary political parties.
